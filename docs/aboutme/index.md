I am Maxwell McFarlane.

As an engineer I am the person you go to when you have a problem and need some way to solve it. My duty as an engineer is to take any problem presented to me and find a way to improve it or reconfigure it with less cost or risk. The thing that brought me to mechanical engineering is first seeing 3D printers be introduced. I've always had a great fascination for 3D printers because I felt they hold a lot of potential for the benefits of the future. There are already many documented and reported cases of 3D printers being used on almost everything in every field. One large 3D printer could print concrete into the shape of a house. Another 3D printer is a fine needle that pierces a gelatin mold that creates molds in a stable suspended space without the need of something to keep it in the air like supports or wires. There has even been a case where a mosquito proboscis had been hardened with resign and attached as the nozzle to a 3D printer to make such small, detailed structures that were millimeters tall and still retained all the details of the original blueprints. I seek to work with these great devices and further explore their vast usefulness for the benefit of humankind.

Extra Details (optional):
I have worked on a few small-scale projects from various classes, but I think the one that always stuck was from one intro-level engineering class where me and a team of 3 other people were tasked with using paper to create a tower that met a certain height limit. We had fake money for a budget, guidelines to follow as if we were making a real blueprint, and even a warning of a heavy fine if we built under the requested limit and another heavy fine if the tower fell over in a "Wind Test" against his classroom fan. It was a big competition against 5 other teams and every detail counted. Even scotch tape and cutting the paper meant spending some of your budget. Everyone tried their absolute hardest to try and save the most money while having the highest tower that by the end my table placed 2nd, which we were proud of. We had designed the tower with folds and bends instead of cuts and tape, so through origami we saved almost as much as the winning team who used geometry to win. After class, I talked to the professor about what the best answer was. He smiled, took a singular paper and crumbled it into a ball and placed it on the table with a single piece of tape. He explained that everyone tried brainstorming such elaborate ideas that even the most ridiculous answer passed. The paper ball broke the heavy fine of being too short, but it passed the wind test so by technicality by using near to nothing he saved the most money than some elaborate tower system like everyone designed. I think the morale of that story will forever be with me that not all solutions require a brainstorm, just basic analysis and a step back to understanding the priorities.

To me, **"What does it mean to defend an engineering decision: and do you currently know how to do it?"** is a lot like asking a lawyer to defend his client. I believe that if everything was accounted for with all possible criteria and precautions followed, then defending an engineering decision could be simply reasoning why this decision is better than others and why other reasons wouldn't be as effective/useful/practical/etcetera to the given project. I would say to be able to do it you need to have two skills, debate skills to give good counter arguments why the engineering decision made was what's best and the facts to support it, and critical thinking to see how this decision will play out in the future and the effects it will have on the project or people surrounding the project.
